## Start with the real problem, not the assigned one

The brief and the bottleneck are often different things, and the distance between them is where most
of the value sits. It is also the part of the job nobody assigns you.

**My roadmap said engagement. The app took fifteen seconds to open.** A feature shipped onto a
fifteen-second launch is only ever measured on the people patient enough to wait, which is a smaller
and far more forgiving group than the one the feature was designed for — so whatever numbers came
back would not really have been about the feature.

The test I use is narrower than *is the technical debt bad.* It is whether the floor is currently
corrupting the measurement, so that you could not read the result of the next thing you ship even if
you shipped it. At fifteen seconds you cannot.

The failure mode on the other side is real, and worth naming: a team can spend a quarter on
foundations, ship nothing anyone outside the building can see, and call it maturity.

## Use evidence before instinct

Instinct picks the option you already like. Evidence tells you which options the problem actually
has.

**Cohort analysis put a drop-off on the table instead of a feature request**, and three retention
strategies were live options rather than one preference with two straw men beside it. Content,
incentives, competition — all three had advocates, and there was appetite for roughly one.

What decided it was not which sounded best in a room. It was asking what each one costs in the
twelfth week rather than the first.

## Make the trade-off explicit

A decision with no cost was not a decision. And there is a real difference between naming the cost
at the time and naming it afterwards: **a trade-off named at the time is judgment. The same
trade-off named afterwards is a defence.**

Choosing competition meant accepting that a standings table is a daily notice to whoever is last —
and in a health product, the people most likely to be last are often the people the product exists
for. That belongs in the same sentence as the choice.

The same applies to what I did not own. Every case study on this site says what engineering owned,
what I did not decide, and where a number’s attribution stops. That is not modesty. Bounding a claim
is what makes the unbounded ones worth anything.

## Measure what shipped, and say what it proves

A number without its population is a claim. A number with one is evidence — and the limits are part
of the finding rather than a disclaimer at the end of it.

**Session time moved 3.5 to 7.8 minutes, and it is a contestable north star in a health app.** A
product that works can shorten a session: open it, log the thing, leave. Time in app is a reasonable
metric for a competitive feature whose loop is checking where you stand, and a poor one for a
product whose job is to get you outside. Both are true at once, which is why the number needs an
argument next to it rather than a bigger typeface.

## What I would do differently

Three things, taken from the three case studies rather than invented for this page.

**I would instrument before I pick the metric.** On the league I chose a north star the existing
instrument could produce, rather than the one the question needed — a week-two-to-week-four return
rate on people who joined a league against people who did not. Choosing the metric second is how you
end up defending it later.

**I would build the guardrails into the launch plan.** A retention mechanic that burns notification
permission is a loss wearing a win’s clothes. Notification opt-outs, uninstalls and quiet season
attrition are counter-metrics, and counter-metrics are worth nothing discovered afterwards.

**I would treat a performance win as a position, not a state.** Under two seconds is not somewhere
you arrive; it is somewhere you hold, and the next three sprints of feature work are where it goes
back. The size ceiling belongs in the build on week one, so the win stops depending on anyone
remembering it.

## The first five numbers I would instrument

Everything above happened on one product surface at one employer, which is a fair thing for a
reader to be suspicious of. So: a new consumer product, week one, nothing instrumented yet. These
are the five I would put in first, and they are deliberately boring.

1. **Activation, defined as the first time the product does its job** — not signup, not onboarding
   completion. For a health app that is the first useful reading in front of the user; for a
   marketplace it is the first search that returns something worth clicking. Everything downstream
   is uninterpretable until this one is defined, because every retention curve is a curve of people
   who reached *something* and you have to say what.
2. **Week-1 → week-4 return rate, by activation cohort.** Not DAU. DAU moves when marketing spends
   and tells you nothing about whether the product works.
3. **Time to first value, at the median and the 90th percentile.** The median tells you the design
   works. The 90th tells you who the design abandoned, and that is usually the larger group.
4. **The one action that correlates with week-4 return**, found rather than assumed. Every product
   has one and it is rarely the one on the roadmap. This is the only number on the list that
   requires analysis rather than instrumentation.
5. **A guardrail I would be embarrassed to miss** — notification opt-outs, uninstalls, support
   volume per thousand actives. Cheap to log, and the thing that catches a win bought with something
   you cannot get back.

**What I would deliberately not measure yet:** anything about revenue attribution, anything
requiring a funnel definition longer than one sentence, and NPS. Not because they do not matter, but
because instrumenting them in week one produces numbers precise enough to argue about and too early
to be true, and an organisation that starts arguing about a bad number is slower than one with no
number at all.

**What this artifact is missing, and I would rather say it than let the omission pass as tidiness:
who objected.** The record captures what was decided and why. It does not capture who pushed back,
on what grounds, or how it resolved — and a prioritisation table with no unhappy person in it is
only half of what prioritisation actually is. That is a real gap in my own record rather than a
gap in this page, and it is the first thing I would fix about how I document decisions.
