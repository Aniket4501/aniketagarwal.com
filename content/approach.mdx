Three beliefs, each with the work attached and the part of it I would argue about. This page is argument; the record is under [Work](/work), where every number carries its population and method, and the ones I cannot qualify are marked instead of filled in.

## Fix the floor before you build the ceiling

**The roadmap was engagement. The app took fifteen seconds to open, against a 2s benchmark, and an engagement feature shipped onto a fifteen-second launch is only ever measured on the people who waited.**

Those people are a smaller and far more patient group than the one the feature was designed for. Whatever numbers come back, they are not really about the feature.

I prioritised launch time instead. We shipped it under two seconds in eight weeks, and the bundle came down from 25MB to 6MB along the way.

The failure mode on the other side of this is real: a team spends a quarter on foundations, ships nothing anyone outside the building can see, and calls it maturity. So the test is narrower than "is the tech debt bad". It is whether the floor is corrupting the measurement, so that the next feature's result would be unreadable even if you shipped it. At fifteen seconds it is.

Read [Two Seconds](/work/two-seconds) for the eight weeks this comes from.

## A decision with no cost was not a decision

**Three retention strategies were on the table: content, incentives, gamification. I prioritised competitive mechanics, and that means accepting that a leaderboard is a reason to open the app for the person who is winning and a reason to delete it for the person who is last.**

Content and incentives buy attention you have to keep paying for. Another article, another voucher, next week, forever. Competition manufactures the reason to come back out of other users, which is the only engine of the three with no recurring invoice attached to it.

That is the argument for. The argument against is the same sentence from the other end: a competition sorts a user base into people who like being ranked and people who feel worse for it, and in a health product the second group is not a rounding error.

Which is why the cost belongs in the same sentence as the choice. A trade-off named at the time is a decision. The same trade-off named afterwards is a defence.

Read [Steps Premier League](/work/steps-premier-league) for what shipped and what it gave up.

## A metric you cannot defend is a metric you do not own

**Session time was the declared north star for that launch, and it moved 3.5 → 7.8 min. Session time is a contestable north star in a health app, and I would rather argue about it in public than quietly swap it for something safer.**

The objection is fair. A health product that works can shorten sessions: open it, log the thing, leave. Time in app is a reasonable metric for a competitive feature whose loop is checking where you stand, and a poor one for a product whose job is to get you outdoors. Both are true at once, which is why the number needs an argument beside it and not a bigger typeface.

What it cannot tell you is whether anybody came back. The number I would want next to it is week-2 to week-4 return, measured on the people who joined a league against the same weeks for people who did not.

Which is also why session time and DAU appear here with their population, window and method left as open questions instead of filled in with the most flattering reading available.

## The postmortem is the section I cannot write yet

## Manual logging is the ceiling on health-app engagement in India

A point of view, not a case study. None of what follows is work I have shipped.

Every engagement mechanic in a consumer health app is eventually competing with a person's willingness to type. Steps are the exception, and not because step challenges are clever: the phone counts steps whether or not anyone opens the app. The moment a loop needs a number only the user can supply — a meal, a reading, a home test — it acquires a daily tax.

India has a structural answer that most markets do not. Under the [Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission](https://abdm.gov.in), a person holds an ABHA identifier and can consent to their health records being shared between registered providers and applications. A lab report can arrive in an app because someone consented once, rather than because they photographed it on a kitchen table. **That changes what the product is allowed to open on.** The first screen stops being a form.

The objections are stronger than the pitch, and there are three worth stating. Consent is a funnel: linking records means handing over a medical history on the strength of whatever trust the app has earned by signup, which is close to none, and the pull is most valuable exactly when the app has least earned it. Coverage decides whether the promise survives contact, because a pull returns only what a producer has published to the network, and an app that promises a complete history and returns two documents is worse off than one that promised nothing. And an identifier is not a user: a number created at a hospital registration desk and a person who knows they have one are different populations, so any adoption figure has to be read for which it counts.

There is a retention figure for ABDM-linked cohorts circulating in Indian healthtech writing. No uncited benchmark goes on this site, and that one has no study I can link to, so it is not here.

What would settle it is a funnel, not a benchmark: what share of people who begin the linking flow finish it, what share of them have anything worth pulling, and what those people do in the fortnight afterwards. Until someone publishes that, everyone arguing this — me included — is arguing from mechanism.

Getting the data is also the easy half. Pulling a verified report solves acquisition and says nothing about whether the person holding it can read it, which is the problem I went and built [Grounded](/lab/grounded) for.
