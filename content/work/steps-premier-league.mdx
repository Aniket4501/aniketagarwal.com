## A step counter competes with the one already on the phone

The app served a registered base above a million people, and session time sat at 3.5 minutes. Cohort
analysis was the input to what happened next, and it is why this was a decision and not a feature
request.

There is a week-2 drop-off in my record, but it sits against a different initiative — the engagement
suite — and fusing the two lines would be me telling a tidier story than the one I have.

[NEEDS: week-2 finding — this league, the suite, or both?]

The structural problem underneath is not specific to this app. A phone already counts steps: in the
background, for free, on the lock screen, with nothing to open and nothing to log into. **Any step
feature inside a health app is competing with a widget the user never had to open**, and "come and see
the number your operating system already showed you" is a request, not a reason.

## Two of the three options are a bill that never stops arriving

Content, incentives and gamification arrive at a review looking like three things to scope: estimate
the effort, guess the lift, pick the best ratio. I do not think that is what they are.

**Content and incentives are subscriptions the company pays, not the user.** Content means somebody
produces new material every week, forever, and the day production slows the reason to return slows
with it. Incentives mean a reward keeps arriving; withdraw it and the behaviour it bought leaves too,
and the user has now been told exactly what that behaviour was worth.

Competition works differently. The mechanic is built once and the fuel comes from other people. The
recurring cost moves onto the users, and they pay it willingly, because what they are buying is
standing.

Not features.

**The question that settles this is which of the three still works in a quarter when nobody is funding
it.** That comparison decides the answer well before you reach effort estimates.

## The benefit a health app sells arrives too late to change what you do tonight

The method was cohort analysis. **The sample, the window and the shape of the curve are not things I
can publish, so I am not going to draw them** — a cohort chart with plausible-looking values and a
blank axis is a worse artifact than no chart at all. What survives without the values is the argument.

Walking has a payoff. It arrives as a number at a check-up years from now, or as something that
quietly fails to happen to you in your fifties. None of that lands this evening. **A product whose
promised benefit has a feedback cadence measured in decades has to borrow a loop that runs at the
cadence of a day**, or the user has no signal to respond to between install and the next annual
reminder.

Competition is the cheapest borrowed loop there is. Dropping two places overnight is felt tomorrow
morning: a small, faintly ridiculous, entirely dependable signal, arriving at exactly the frequency
the behaviour underneath needs and cannot generate for itself.

## Three options, and the one I would have been safest choosing

The safe answer was incentives. Nobody argues with them in a review: the mechanism is legible, the
lift shows up quickly, and if it underperforms you get to say the reward was too small rather than
that the idea was wrong. That last property is why I distrust it. A strategy whose failure mode is
always "we did not spend enough" teaches you nothing.

The dimension that usually goes missing is reversibility, and not in the sense of "can it be switched
off". Everything can be switched off. Reversibility is what it costs to switch off in front of the
people who were using it. Content decays quietly and almost nobody can name the week it ended. A
withdrawn reward reads as a downgrade. A league is the worst of the three on that dimension, because
standings are a public record and a season abandoned mid-table is a promise broken in front of an
audience.

**I chose the least reversible option, and I would rather have that written down in advance than
discovered in week nine.**

## I shipped competition, and it costs the people who do not want to compete

**I evaluated three ways to fix the drop-off and shipped competition, because content and incentives
buy attention you have to keep paying for, and competition manufactures a reason to open the app on
the day your own motivation is gone.**

What that buys is a mechanic that funds itself. What it costs is a population.

A leaderboard does not read as neutral. To someone who is unwell, or deconditioned, or simply slower
than the people they were placed beside, a standings table is a daily notice that they are losing at
something they never entered to win. I would expect that group to overlap heavily with the people a
health product exists for, which is what makes the cost worth naming instead of absorbing.

Be exact about the limit of that paragraph, though. Accepting the cost knowingly is a different thing
from never having thought about it, and what the cost actually came to is a second sentence that needs
data behind it.

## Session time went 3.5 to 7.8 minutes. Here is what that establishes.

Session time moved from 3.5 minutes to 7.8 minutes, and my own record names it as the North Star
Metric for this work. I show the two values with no percentage beside them. The division takes four
seconds and I would rather you did it than took my rounding on trust.

**That pair of numbers establishes that people who were in the app stayed in it longer. It does not
establish that more people came back.** The second is the question the drop-off actually asked.

I attribute the movement directionally, not causally.

<Drawer label="How I would define session time before quoting it again">

Three things move this number without anything changing in the product, and I would want all three
settled before the figure is quoted again.

Median or mean. A leaderboard produces a long tail of very engaged users, and a mean follows that tail
wherever it goes.

What ends a session. A thirty-second inactivity timeout and a five-minute one are different metrics
sharing a name.

Who is in the denominator. Everyone who opened the app, or everyone who joined a league — and that one
matters most, because league joiners are self-selected toward engagement, so measuring the mechanic on
them is the most flattering reading available.

</Drawer>

## An engagement win that burns notification permission is a loss

A competitive mechanic has a failure mode that looks identical to success on a session-time chart. It
works by manufacturing urgency, urgency is delivered by notification, and notification permission is
not renewable — one revocation per user, permanent. A league that lifts session time this quarter by
pushing four times a day has sold a durable asset to buy a temporary number. The dashboard shows the
win. The cost does not appear on the same dashboard, or in the same quarter.

Which is why the counter-metrics have to be agreed before launch: opt-out and system-level permission
revocation per cohort per week, and uninstalls split by whether the user ever joined a league.

## Session time was the declared North Star. I would argue for a different instrument now.

The case for it is real and I will make it first. The mechanic's theory is that people come back to
check standings; checking standings is a session; if the theory holds the number moves, and if it
fails it sits still. Sensitive, fast, readable by everyone in the room without a briefing.

The case against is stronger. **In a health app, a product working well can reduce session time.** Log
the walk, see the number, close the app, go outside. A session that shortened because the flow
improved and a session that shortened because the user gave up look identical in the aggregate.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="The instrument I would argue for instead">

**A cohort return rate: of the people who joined a league in a given week, what share opened the app
in each of the following weeks, against the same curve for people who did not join.** It answers the
question that was actually asked. It is slower, far less flattering, and it has to be defined before
launch, because a cohort assembled afterwards is one assembled knowing the answer.

Session time was the declared North Star and I am not going to pretend otherwise on a page read beside
my resume. It was the wrong instrument for the question underneath, and picking the instrument before
the mechanic is the part I would sequence differently.

</Callout>
