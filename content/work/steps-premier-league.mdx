## A step counter competes with the phone in your pocket

Cohort analysis put a drop-off on the table. Not a feature request from anyone — a shape in the data
that said people were arriving and then not coming back.

The underlying problem is structural, and it is not specific to this app. **Your phone already
counts your steps. It tells you the number for free, on the lock screen, without anyone opening
anything.** A health app that shows you the same figure has to answer a question the operating
system has already answered better.

## Three strategies were live options. Only one had no recurring bill.

Content, incentives, gamification. All three were real, all three had advocates, and there was
appetite for roughly one.

**Content and incentives buy attention you have to keep paying for.** Another article, another
voucher, next week, forever. Competition manufactures the reason to come back out of other users,
which makes it the only one of the three whose cost is the mechanic rather than the fuel.

<Callout variant="tradeoff" label="What the choice costs">

A leaderboard is a reason to open the app for the person who is winning and a reason to delete it
for the person who is last. That is not a footnote to the decision, it is the decision — and in a
health product, the people most likely to be last are frequently the people the product exists for.

**A decision with no cost was not a decision.** I would rather put this in the same sentence as the
choice than in a retrospective.

</Callout>

## What shipped

A recurring, time-boxed league built on step data the phone already collects — cohorts, standings, a
season that ends and restarts. The design insight is in the restart: a league with no season end has
no urgency, and one with no restart has no second chance for anybody who fell behind.

## What happened

Session time moved from 3.5 to 7.8 minutes. That was the declared north star for the launch.

**It is also a contestable north star in a health app, and I would rather argue about it here than
quietly swap it for something safer.** A health product that works can *shorten* a session: open it,
log the thing, leave. Time in app is a reasonable metric for a competitive feature whose loop is
checking where you stand, and a poor one for a product whose job is to get you out of the app and
onto a walk. Both are true at once, which is why the number needs an argument beside it rather than
a bigger typeface.

## What I would do differently

**I would instrument the cohort before picking the metric, not after.** What session time cannot
tell you is whether anybody came back. The number I would want next to it is a week-two-to-week-four
return rate, measured on people who joined a league against the same weeks for people who did not —
and choosing the north star before that instrument existed meant choosing the metric the existing
instrument could produce.

The second thing is guardrails. A retention mechanic that burns notification permission is a loss
wearing a win's clothes, and the counter-metrics that would catch it — notification opt-outs,
uninstalls, how many people quietly stop entering a season — belong in the launch plan rather than
in the post-mortem.
