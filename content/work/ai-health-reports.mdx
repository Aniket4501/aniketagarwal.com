## I built a report for a reader who was not the customer

A consumer health app with 1M+ registered users. I built the AI Health Reports on it 0→1: the product requirements, the UX flow, and the personalisation logic that decides what an individual report says.

**A report like this is opened by one person and paid for by another, and almost every hard decision inside it comes out of that gap.**

My record confirms the commercial end of that structure — 5+ enterprise clients — and not the channel. Employer health plans, insurers, hospitals, direct signup: the assumption running underneath this whole case study is that the reader and the payer are different parties, and it is stated once, here, as an assumption. [NEEDS: who pays, and how do readers arrive?]

## On paper this was a personalisation project

Read the scope back and it is a consumer brief: requirements, a flow, and the logic that decides what your report says about you. Build that well and the person who opens it gets something worth opening. The framing is not wrong. It is incomplete, and the incompleteness is where the money was.

**The beneficiary reads the report, the employer buys the programme, and the report is the thing that makes the programme legible at renewal — which is how a consumer feature ended up inside five enterprise closes.**

Three parties, three unrelated tests. The reader's test is whether the report tells them something about themselves they could not already see, in language they can act on. The buyer's test is not a smaller version of that one: they will likely never open a single report, and they are deciding whether to renew a programme whose value is otherwise invisible to them. And in any product that says something about a person's health, somebody has to be willing to put their name to what it says. That third test is a veto rather than a preference. It does not make the product better, it decides whether the product is allowed to exist.

Most consumer PMs never have to hold all three at once. When the reader is the buyer, the useful artifact and the sellable artifact are the same object and you get the second one free.

Here they come apart.

## What a report should say is decided by what the reader can do next

Personalisation logic sounds like a ranking problem. **It is a series of decisions about what to say to a person who did not ask you a question.**

Every one of those decisions needs the same input: what this reader is in a position to do about it. A result nobody can act on is not information, it is anxiety with a timestamp. Health results in particular arrive in units most people cannot interpret, and the distance between holding a number and knowing what it means about you is the entire reason an artifact like this gets funded.

What I will not do is reconstruct the research. My published record contains the scope of this work and not its method — no interview count, no sample, no instrument — and a case study that invents those reads worse to this audience than one that says it does not have them.

One unanswered question decides most of the rest. Personalisation over self-reported data is a different product from personalisation over lab results, and different again over wearable data. The first is a summary. The second carries an interpretation burden, and everything further down this page moves depending on which one it was. [NEEDS: which of those do the reports consume?]

## Where a commercial ask can sit inside a health report

The same artifact carries two things: an interpretation of somebody's health, and an ask. That is a genuine product problem with no comfortable answer, because **the placement that converts best is the placement that costs the most trust, and trust is the whole asset.** A report nobody believes is worth nothing to the reader, and one renewal later it is worth nothing to the buyer either.

Reversibility is the column most people leave out, and here it is the one that decides the argument. Pulling a section takes a deploy. Pulling a reader's conclusion that your health report is a sales surface takes considerably longer, and you will not know you need to until the number that would have told you has already moved.

Which of the three shipped is not in my published record, and I am not going to fill it in from memory on a page whose whole argument is that I state things carefully. [NEEDS: which placement shipped, and what did you rule out?]

## The decision in the record is the bigger one

What my record does say is that I created the cross-sell hooks. That is the prior decision and it is the larger one: this report would ask the reader for something.

What I gave up is the version that asks for nothing. That report is easier to trust, easier to defend to anyone who has to approve it, and it earns nothing — which inside a programme that has to be renewed is not a neutral outcome.

<Callout variant="tradeoff" label="What the hooks cost">
  The only revenue number attached to this work attaches to the hooks, not to the reports. So the most commercially legible part of the product is also its most trust-expensive part. That is not an accident of this project, it is the shape of the problem: anything that converts inside a health artifact converts because the reader is in a receptive state, and the reason they are receptive is that they were reading something they believed.
</Callout>

## The report is an event, not a surface

What shipped: the reports, 0→1, with the requirements, the flow and the personalisation logic behind them, and the hooks inside them. There are no screenshots here — the product is not mine to publish.

A report generated from a periodic input regenerates on the cadence of that input. **If the cadence is annual, the artifact is an event, and an event has to survive the fifty-one weeks in which it is not happening.** There are two honest answers and they are different products. Either the report becomes a surface that moves against something that changes daily, at which point it has stopped being a report; or it stays an event, the app around it carries the liveness, and the report is designed to be re-opened rather than re-generated. I am not claiming I solved that.

## Two outcomes, and one of them is not mine

My record attributes 15% incremental revenue to the cross-sell hooks. Not to the reports — and I hold that boundary because it is the boundary a sceptic draws for me in the first minute anyway. It does not say 15% of what line, measured over what period, or attributed by what method, which is why the figure sits at the top of this page with all three questions still attached to it.

The reports were cited as a key USP in closing 5+ enterprise clients. **I built the artifact; I did not own the sale.** A deal closes for a dozen reasons and a differentiator named in a close is not a differentiator that caused it. I attribute this directionally, not causally.

There is a sharper way to say what is missing here. The buyer's read on this product exists, because renewals and closes get reported to you whether or not you ask for them. The reader's read does not exist in my record at all.

## The instrument I did not have would have measured the reader

Both numbers here are on the buyer's side, and both are lagging and contaminated — revenue moves with pricing and with a sales motion I did not run, and closes move with everything.

The read that would settle whether the report worked sits on the other side, and it is cheap. Two things would do it: whether the reader takes the specific next step their own report recommends, broken out by what the report said, and whether they can restate what it told them a week later. The first is an action rate you can instrument in a sprint. The second needs people and a fortnight. Neither requires randomising anything, because the question is not whether the report improved a health outcome — it is whether the report was understood and acted on, which is a much lower bar and a far more useful one.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="What this traded away">
  The commercial read arrives whether or not you build it. The reader's read only exists if somebody builds it. So the default outcome of an artifact with a split audience is that you learn a great deal about the buyer and very little about the person it was written for — and the shape of my record is exactly that. It is structural rather than an oversight, which is the reason to design against it up front instead of resolving to try harder.
</Callout>

## The first question an AI-PM interviewer asks about this is not in my record

Ask anyone who has shipped generative features in a regulated domain about a report like this and the first question is always the same one: how does it avoid giving medical advice? It is the right question, and it is not a compliance question. A sentence that explains a result and a sentence that prescribes a response to it are grammatically almost identical, and a model that is good at the first is good at the second by accident.

**I am not going to answer that with an architecture I cannot show you.**

A boundary like that can be enforced in the prompt, in deterministic rules around the model, in fixed templates, or by a person reading the output. Where mine sat is the one thing an AI-PM reader most wants from this page, and it is not in the record. [NEEDS: prompt, rules, templates, review, or none of them?]

If the answer is any of the first four, this is the strongest case study on the site and it belongs at the front of it. Until that answer arrives, it sits third and says why.

What does exist is a tool. [Grounded](/lab/grounded) scores generated health text on four things: whether every claim traces to the input it was given, whether it steps outside its scope, whether it hands off when it should, and whether an ordinary reader can follow it. I built it for this portfolio, not at work — it is labelled that way on its own page — and it exists because the question above deserves a real answer and this case study does not contain one.
