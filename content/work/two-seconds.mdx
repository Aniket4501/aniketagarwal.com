## The app took fifteen seconds to open. The benchmark was two.

HCL Healthcare's health app served more than a million registered users. I had defined the product
roadmap, and what was on it was engagement: reasons to open a health app in a week when nothing about
your health has changed.

Then I timed the launch.

Fifteen seconds.

**Against a two-second benchmark, that number is the price of admission to everything else on the
roadmap.** Someone who taps the icon and waits fifteen seconds is not forming an opinion about your
streaks feature. They are forming one about whether the app works.

## Latency sat in the engineering column. Adoption sat on my roadmap.

The fifteen seconds were already known. They sat where slow things sit, next to refactors and platform
upgrades, competing for engineering time against other engineering work. Classified that way it is a
quality problem, and quality problems lose to deadlines for years.

I reclassified it, and not because I could fix it. I could not have written any of that code. What a
problem is filed under decides what it gets compared against, and a latency number owned by product
competes against features — the only comparison in which its real value is visible.

**The roadmap I owned was an engagement roadmap. I spent eight weeks on launch time instead, because
every engagement feature shipped onto that foundation would have been measured on users who had
already left.**

That argument is falsifiable, which is the part of it I trust. If launch time was not what blocked
adoption, then eight weeks of my roadmap bought two impressive numbers and nothing else.

## The churn happened before the first analytics event fired

Here is the property that makes this a case study and not a ticket.

A funnel begins at the first event the app emits, and the app cannot emit one until it has finished
launching. Everyone who abandoned during those fifteen seconds is missing from the denominator. They
are an absence rather than a drop-off, because a drop-off needs two measured points and these people
never produced the first — and you have to suspect an absence before you can go looking for it.

Which is also why I cannot show you the chart that would make this section persuasive. The instrument
that would have counted those people is the thing that was broken.

There is no user research behind this and I am not going to invent any. The argument runs from the
number and from what the instrument could and could not see.

<Drawer label="Why you cannot A/B test a latency fix, and what you compare instead">

Randomisation is what makes an A/B test work, and device capability breaks it. A phone's processor,
its free storage and how much memory the other apps on it are holding decide what a cold start costs,
and none of them is assignable. You can randomise which build a device receives. You cannot randomise
the device.

So the instrument degrades, and it is worth being precise about how far. A staged rollout holding the
old build back controls for seasonality, but not for whichever devices happened to receive the new
build first. A pre/post comparison inside a fixed device tier controls for capability, but not for
anything else that moved that week.

Neither is a clean causal read. Saying which one you ran, and what it fails to control for, costs
nothing. Being caught not knowing costs the rest of the page.

One thing about the statistic itself, whichever design produced it. A mean launch time follows the
fast tail, and abandonment lives in the slow one, so a mean that improves while the slow tail sits
still will look exactly like a win.

</Drawer>

## Three ways to spend eight weeks

Reversibility gets read backwards here. Shipping features looks like the reversible choice, since a
feature can be switched off next week. But the eight weeks are not refundable, and every measurement
taken inside them is contaminated: when a feature underperforms on a foundation that loses people
before the first screen, you cannot separate "this feature is weak" from "almost nobody reached it".

Fixing launch time is the least reversible of the three in calendar terms, and I would rather say so
than dress it up.

## I held a roadmap I had written myself

**A decision with no cost is not a decision, and this one cost eight weeks of a roadmap I had defined
myself** — an awkward thing to spend, more so than eight weeks of somebody else's plan.

It cost a second thing. I picked a problem whose success criterion I could not measure cleanly, for
the reason set out in the drawer above, and traded a measurable bet for an unmeasurable one because I
thought the unmeasurable one was larger.

The sceptical reading deserves an answer before anyone asks for it. You fixed something engineering
would have got to eventually. Probably true, but not in eight weeks, and not while it was ranked
against other engineering work by people correctly optimising for engineering throughput. The reframe
did not change who did the work. It changed what the work competed against.

## Under two seconds, and 25MB down to 6MB

Two numbers came out of the eight weeks, and they are displayed differently on purpose.

25MB to 6MB is a 76% reduction computed from two stated values, nothing rounded and nothing inferred:
the only delta on this site that is arithmetic rather than estimate. The precision is the point, not
the size.

Launch time is a different kind of object. What I can stand behind is "under two seconds", an
inequality, and an inequality does not license a percentage — 1.5 and 1.99 imply meaningfully
different reductions from fifteen and my record does not say which. So the page publishes the bound.
At least 7.5× faster holds for every value the inequality permits, and it survives the first question
a sceptic asks.

One caveat I would rather volunteer than be asked about. "Bundle size" is at least three measurements
— what a person downloads, what the install occupies, what the build artifact weighs — and I have not
stated which one those totals are.

## The numbers moved. Here is what they do not prove.

15s to under 2s, and 25MB to 6MB, both inside eight weeks, on an app that was live throughout.

I attribute the adoption argument directionally, not causally. There was no holdback I can publish,
and no clean randomised design was available to build one from. What I have is two physical quantities
about a piece of software, measured before and after a period in which a team worked on them.

**A launch-time fix removes a reason to leave. It does not demonstrate that anyone stayed, and those
are different claims.** The second is the one a Head of Product actually wants, and it is not in what I
can publish.

## What eight weeks of latency work could not buy

<Callout variant="mistake" label="The measurement I did not buy">

Eight weeks bought a foundation and two physical numbers. They did not buy a read on whether the
people who had been bouncing came back, and the window in which that read was cheapest is the window
the fix consumed. The counterfactual only exists while the old build is still out there.

The instrument was never exotic: define a cohort before the change ships, hold the definition fixed,
and read that same cohort's return rate afterwards. Run this again and I would define it up front and
accept a slower start in exchange for an answer. Not because the fix was the wrong call. Because a fix
and the evidence that it worked are two deliverables, and I shipped one of them.

</Callout>

The other thing eight weeks cannot buy is durability, and this is where a performance win usually
dies. A performance budget becomes a product requirement when it has three parts: a stated ceiling, a
named owner, and a gate that stops a release from crossing it. With two of the three it is a
preference, and preferences lose to deadlines. Every dependency added after week eight spends the
budget, nobody is ever the person who broke it, and the number drifts back without anyone having
decided to let it.
