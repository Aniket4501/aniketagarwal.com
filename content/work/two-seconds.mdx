## The app took fifteen seconds to open. The benchmark was two.

HCL Healthcare's health app served more than a million registered users. I had defined the product
roadmap, and what was on it was engagement: reasons to open a health app in a week when nothing
about your health has changed.

Then I looked at how long the app took to launch.

Fifteen seconds.

**Against a two-second benchmark that is not a performance statistic. It is the price of admission to
everything else on the roadmap.** A person who taps the icon and waits fifteen seconds is not forming
an opinion about your streaks feature. They are forming one about whether the app works.

## Latency was on the engineering backlog. Adoption was on my roadmap.

A fifteen-second launch was already known. It sat where slow things sit — in the engineering column,
next to refactors and platform upgrades, competing for engineering time against other engineering
work. Classified that way it is a quality problem, and quality problems lose to deadlines for years.

I reclassified it, and not because I could fix it. I could not have written any of that code. A
problem's category decides what it gets compared against, and a latency number owned by product
competes against features. That is the only comparison in which its real value is visible.

**The roadmap I owned was an engagement roadmap. I spent eight weeks on a fifteen-second launch time
instead, because every engagement feature I shipped onto that foundation would have been measured
against users who had already left.**

That argument is falsifiable, which is the part of it I trust. If launch time was not what blocked
adoption, then eight weeks of my roadmap bought two impressive numbers and nothing else.

## The churn happened before the first analytics event fired

Here is the property that makes this a case study rather than a ticket.

A funnel begins at the first event the app emits, and the app cannot emit one until it has finished
launching. Everyone who abandoned during those fifteen seconds is therefore missing from the
denominator. They are not a drop-off; a drop-off needs two measured points and these people never
produced the first. They are an absence, and you have to suspect an absence to go looking for it.

You cannot fix what your instrumentation never saw. Which is also why I cannot show you the chart
that would make this section persuasive: the instrument that would have counted those people is the
thing that was broken.

There is no user research behind this section and I am not going to invent any. The argument runs
from the number and from what the instrument can and cannot see.

A note on the statistic. A mean launch time is the wrong one here. The mean is pulled toward fast
devices, and fast devices belong to the people who build the app and the people least likely to
leave. Abandonment lives in the tail. A mean that improves while the tail does not is a number that
got better for a population that was never going anywhere, and it will look exactly like a win.

<Drawer label="Why you cannot A/B test a latency fix, and what you compare instead">

Randomisation is the thing that makes an A/B test work. You assign the treatment, and because
assignment is random the two arms differ only in what you assigned. Device capability breaks that. A
phone's processor, its free storage and how much memory the other apps on it are holding are the
variables that decide what a cold start costs, and none of them is assignable. You can randomise
which build a device receives. You cannot randomise the device.

So the instrument degrades, and it is worth being precise about how far. A staged rollout with the
old build held back gives you a contemporaneous comparison: it controls for seasonality, but not for
whichever devices happened to receive the new build first. A pre/post comparison inside a fixed
device tier controls for capability, but not for anything else that moved that week — another
release, a campaign, a festival.

Neither is a clean causal read. Saying which one you ran, and what it fails to control for, costs
nothing. Being caught not knowing costs the rest of the page.

## Three ways to spend eight weeks

Eight weeks could take three shapes. The first was already the plan and needed no argument from
anyone, which is what makes it the dangerous one.

Reversibility is the dimension I want to lean on, because it usually gets read backwards. Shipping
features looks like the reversible choice, since a feature can be turned off next week. But the eight
weeks are not refundable, and every measurement taken inside them is contaminated: when a feature
underperforms on a foundation that loses people before the first screen, you cannot separate "this
feature is weak" from "almost nobody reached it". You lose the time and you lose the ability to learn
from it.

Fixing launch time is the least reversible of the three in calendar terms, and I would rather say so
than dress it up. What it buys is durability: a foundation, once fixed, does not need re-deciding.
It does need defending, which is the last section.

## I held a roadmap I had written myself

I chose the third option. **A decision with no cost is not a decision, and this one cost eight weeks
of a roadmap I had defined myself — a more awkward thing to spend than eight weeks of someone else's
plan.**

It cost a second thing. I picked a problem whose success criterion I could not measure cleanly, for
the reason set out in the drawer above: device capability is not randomisable, so the clean
instrument was never available. I traded a measurable bet for an unmeasurable one because I believed
the unmeasurable one was larger.

The sceptical reading deserves an answer before anyone has to ask. You fixed something engineering
would have got to eventually. Probably true, but not in eight weeks, and not while it was ranked
against other engineering work by people correctly optimising for engineering throughput. The reframe
did not change who did the work. It changed what the work competed against, and that is what a PM is
for.

## Under two seconds, and 25MB down to 6MB

Two numbers came out of the eight weeks, and one of them deserves a note.

25MB to 6MB is a 76% reduction computed from two stated values, nothing rounded and nothing inferred:
the only delta on this site that is arithmetic rather than estimate. I point that out because the
precision is the point rather than the size.

The caveat comes with it. "Bundle size" is at least three different measurements: what a person
downloads, what the install occupies, and what the build artifact weighs. I would rather volunteer
that than be asked.

<Drawer label="Why 25MB to 6MB is the only figure here I show as an exact percentage">

Both ends of that one are stated values, so the reduction is arithmetic: nineteen of twenty-five,
which is 76.0% exactly.

The launch-time number is a different kind of object. What I can stand behind is "under two seconds",
which is an inequality rather than a measurement, and an inequality does not license a percentage.
The record does not say 1.5 or 1.99, and those two imply meaningfully different reductions from
fifteen. Publishing one tidy figure would assert a precision the source does not contain, and the
first person to ask "under two, or exactly what?" would find that out in a single question.

So the page publishes the bound instead: at least 7.5× faster. That is true for every value the
inequality permits, it is more striking than the percentage would have been, and it survives the
question.

This is not fussiness about one number. It is the rule the rest of the page is written under. A
figure is displayed at the precision its source actually supports, and where the source supports
less, the page says less.

</Drawer>

## The numbers moved. Here is what they do not prove.

15s to under 2s, and 25MB to 6MB, both inside eight weeks, on an app that was live throughout.

I attribute the adoption argument directionally, not causally. There was no holdback I can publish,
and per the drawer above no clean randomised design was available to build one from. What I have is
two physical quantities about a piece of software, measured before and after a period in which a team
worked on them.

**A launch-time fix removes a reason to leave. It does not demonstrate that anyone stayed, and those
are different claims.** The second is the one a Head of Product actually wants, and it is not in what
I can publish.

<Drawer label="A launch-time fix cannot tell you whether anyone came back">

Latency is an input to adoption, not a measure of it. Making an app open in under two seconds removes
one reason to abandon it. Whether the people who had been abandoning it came back is a separate
question with a separate instrument.

That instrument is not exotic. Define a cohort before the change ships, hold the definition fixed,
and read the same cohort's return rate afterwards, on a device population that did not itself change
between the two reads. It is a modest piece of analytics work, and it is the whole difference between
"we made it faster" and "the people who were bouncing came back".

I do not have that read to publish. I would rather write that sentence here than have it drawn out of
me in an interview, because the version where I claim the second thing and cannot produce the cohort
is much worse than the version where I say which claim the evidence supports.

</Drawer>

## What eight weeks of latency work could not buy

<Callout variant="mistake" label="The measurement I did not buy">

Eight weeks bought a foundation and two physical numbers. They did not buy a read on whether the
people who had been bouncing came back, and the window in which that read was cheapest is the window
the fix consumed. The counterfactual only exists while the old build is still out there.

Run again, I would define the cohort before the fix rather than after, and accept a slower start in
exchange for an answer. Not because the fix was the wrong call. Because a fix and the evidence that
it worked are two deliverables, and I shipped one of them.

</Callout>

The other thing eight weeks cannot buy is durability, and this is where a performance win usually
dies. A performance budget becomes a product requirement when it has three parts: a stated ceiling, a
named owner, and a gate that stops a release from crossing it. With two of the three it is a
preference, and preferences lose to deadlines. Every dependency added after week eight spends the
budget, nobody is ever the person who broke it, and the number drifts back without anyone having
decided to let it.
